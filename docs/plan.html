01 — the fork in the road

Everything hinges on the rendering engine — and its licence

“Lightweight, cross-platform, many formats, superfast” is not four features; it is one decision made four times. If you embed a browser engine you lose small and fast. If you write your own PDF parser you lose two years. The realistic answer is a native UI shell wrapped around one battle-tested C rendering library, and the entire shape of the product follows from which library you pick.

The choice

MuPDF gives you PDF, EPUB, MOBI, FB2, CBZ, XPS, SVG, HTML, TXT and raster images from a single dependency, at ~4–6 MB compiled in, and it is the fastest of the mainstream engines — but it is AGPL-3.0. PDFium (Chrome's engine, BSD-3) is battle-tested on dirty real-world files but does only PDF. hayro is the new third answer: a pure-Rust, Apache/MIT PDF rasteriser with no C toolchain at all. Pick your licence posture first, then the engine is decided for you.

04 — office & open document formats

Office files: five honest tiers, one lie to avoid

.docx, .xlsx, .pptx, .odt, .ods and .odp are not opaque binary blobs — they are zip archives of XML. That moves them from “impossible” to “possible, with a caveat you have to put in the UI”. The caveat is layout: a Word page is the output of a layout engine, and you would be writing a second one. So the question is not can you render them, it is what do you promise when you do.

TierWhat the user seesHow it is builtCostVerdict

docx, odt, rtf, and the rest of the text formats		 Text, headings, lists, tables, images, bold/italic/underline, hyperlinks — in a single reflowed column that you can resize and re-zoom like an EPUB. The lie to avoid

Never draw a reflowed DOCX inside a page-shaped white rectangle with a shadow and call it a document preview. It looks like Word, so people will trust the line breaks, the page count and the table widths — and then discover the PDF they export is a different document. Give read mode its own chrome: full-width text, a resize handle, and one honest line at the top — “Reflowed view · layout approximated · Ctrl+Shift+O for exact rendering”.

Treat as a fast T4, not a replacement for T1.
If you do build T4

LibreOffice headless costs roughly 2–3 seconds per document, and a cold start can be far worse; batching amortises it to about 100 ms per file, and a resident listener removes the startup entirely. Two traps: two soffice processes cannot share a user profile (the second exits silently with no output), and if the fonts used by the document are not installed, the layout silently shifts. Cache the converted PDF and invalidate on mtime, or you will pay that cost on every open.

06 — architecture

The hot path, end to end

Speed is not an optimisation phase at the end; it is this pipeline, built once, correctly. The rule: the UI thread never blocks on decode, and the renderer never draws more than it can see.

step 01
Launch
Parse argv, check single-instance socket, create window with a dark clear colour — visible in ~40 ms.
winit · single instance
step 02
Open
mmap the file, sniff the magic bytes (never trust the extension), hand a handle to the engine.
memmap2 · file-type
step 03
Layout
Read page count and page boxes, build a virtual scroll height. No rasterising yet.
Document trait
step 04
Tile render
Worker threads rasterise only visible tiles: draft DPI while scrolling, final DPI when still. Generation counter cancels stale work.
rayon · LRU cache
step 05
Present
Upload finished tiles to the GPU, draw, sleep. A still window should request zero frames.
wgpu · on-demand

COREOne trait, many formats

  • Document trait — page_count, page_size, render(page, rect, dpi), text(page). Every format implements it.
  • A router in front of it — extension and magic bytes → pipeline, with heavy pipelines loaded only when hit. This is how one binary can claim 200 extensions without paying for them at startup (§16).
  • No format logic in the UI. A new format must never touch the viewer.
  • Render cancellation by generation id — a fast flick must not build a queue.
  • One engine instance per window — PDFium is not thread-safe and MuPDF contexts are not shareable. hayro is the exception: it is happy on a worker pool.

MEMORYThe budget that keeps it small

  • LRU tile cache capped in bytes (default ~256 MB), not in pages.
  • Zero-copy: mmap the file, let the engine read from the mapping.
  • No page ever fully rasterised at high DPI unless it is on screen.
  • Drop thumbnails when the sidebar is closed.

UIChrome that disappears

  • No toolbar. A floating page chip and a fade-out scrollbar.
  • Tab strip only when ≥2 tabs; pinned tabs sort left with a dot.
  • Keys: Space/PgDn page, +/- zoom, 1 fit width, 2 fit page, F11 fullscreen, Ctrl+W close, Ctrl+P pin.
  • Light/dark follows the OS, and the page background dims in dark mode instead of inverting the document.

PLATFORMThe thin, ugly layer

  • Windows: registry verbs, RegisterHotKey, Shell_NotifyIcon.
  • macOS: Info.plist types, Carbon hotkey, NSStatusItem.
  • Linux: .desktop + MimeType, X11 grab / Wayland portal, ksni tray.
  • Isolate it behind one platform module with three implementations and graceful no-ops.

Honest note on R7

A truly global “open whatever file is selected in Explorer right now” hotkey is not something the shell offers. What you can build, and what people actually mean, is: press the hotkey → a minimal command palette opens → the path is already in the clipboard or you type three letters → the document opens in 300 ms. Design for that and it will feel like the feature you wanted.

The thing this changes

Your estate is uniformly MIT-with-attribution, zero-dependency, offline-first, single binary. MuPDF would be the first AGPL dependency in the fleet, and AGPL's source obligations sit awkwardly next to a licence whose whole point is a retained attribution watermark. That is the strongest argument in this document for the hayro path: pure Rust, Apache/MIT, no C toolchain, cross-compiles to Windows ARM64 in one command — and it costs you a hand-written EPUB reflow. If you do take MuPDF anyway, the obligation is concrete: the combined work becomes AGPL, and every binary you publish must come with an offer of the complete corresponding source, including your own modifications. Decide that trade deliberately in week one, not in month four.

13 — roadmap

Six milestones, each one shippable

Estimates assume one experienced developer part-time. Every milestone ends with something you can run, not a branch that half-compiles.

M0 · Spikeweek 1kill criteria

Window + one engine + one page of one PDF, timed. If first frame is over 500 ms or the binary is over 30 MB, stop and rethink the stack before writing anything else. Build it on Windows, Linux and ARM64 — cross-compilation pain shows up here or never. Run this spike twice: once with hayro, once with MuPDF, and let the licence argument be settled by a real build rather than by this document.

M1 · PDF viewerweeks 2–4the core

Document trait, virtualised scroll, tile cache with cancellation, zoom ladder, keyboard navigation, password prompt. Benchmark corpus + the §8 numbers in CI. This is the hardest milestone and it is 60% of the value.

M2 · Tabs, windows, pinweek 5the shell

Tab strip, pinned tabs persisted to a small config, multi-window, drag-and-drop, single-instance forwarding, session restore. Deliberately no toolbar.

M3 · The other formatsweeks 6–8breadth

Images and comics first (cheapest, highest delight), then EPUB, XPS, SVG, then plain text with a small highlighter. Each one is a new implementation of the same trait — if any of them touches the UI code, the abstraction is wrong.

M4 · Platform integrationweeks 9–10the part that bites

File associations, right-click verbs, global hotkey (with an honest fallback on Wayland), tray icon, remember-and-restore. Most of this is already written: lift daemon.rs, summon.rs, ipc.rs and the two install-shortcut scripts out of CLDR (§12). Test on a clean VM for each OS — developer machines lie about what is registered.

M5 · Package and shipweeks 11–121.0

Portable zip, MSI, DMG (signed + notarised, or accept the Gatekeeper warning), AppImage + deb. cargo deny in CI, a size budget check on every tag, and a README that states the formats, the non-goals and the licence.

Post-1.0only if asked fordiscipline

In-document search, MOBI/AZW3, DjVu, CHM, HTML — plus the Office read mode and spreadsheet grid (§4) that ship as v1.1 alongside them. Each one is opt-in and each one must not move the startup or size numbers. The moment a feature request starts with “could it also…”, the answer is the app store of your choice.

14 — the honest summary

What you get, and what you do not

Genuinely achievable
  • Under 20 MB, one binary, no runtime
  • PDF + images + comics + EPUB + XPS + SVG + text
  • Office and ODF files in a readable, honestly-labelled mode
  • Sub-300 ms launch, 60 fps on huge documents
  • Tabs, pinned tabs, multiple windows
  • Right-click open on all three desktops
  • Global hotkey on Windows, macOS, X11
  • A tray icon that stays out of the way
Achievable, with a cost
  • Wayland global hotkey — portal only, DE-dependent
  • Windows 11 first-level context menu — needs COM + MSIX identity
  • EPUB typography — MuPDF's reflow is correct but plain
  • macOS distribution — notarisation and $99/yr, or a warning
  • Search — a real feature, not a weekend
Not happening here
  • Office documents in page-accurate layout
  • Office fidelity without a converter or the OS doing the drawing
  • DRM-locked books
  • PostScript / DVI without external converters
  • “Open the Explorer selection” as a true hotkey
  • Annotation, editing, printing pipelines
The one thing to decide today

Not “is it open source” — everything in your estate is. The question is whether this app may carry the only AGPL dependency in the fleet. MuPDF buys you a dozen formats in one binary and the fastest renderer, and costs you a licence that will not sit comfortably beside MIT-with-attribution. hayro keeps the licence story clean and the build pure Rust, and costs you PDF-only plus an EPUB path you write yourself. Both fit behind the same trait — decide with your eyes open, not by accident.

Who owns what — and the gap

SumatraPDF owns Windows fixed-layout. Okular owns Linux all-formats, at KDE's weight. QuickLook owns the Windows spacebar, at 100 MB. Sushi and Maki own their own desktops' preview. peek owns the terminal. Nobody owns small, portable, cross-platform, summoned by a keystroke, read-only. That is not a crowded market with a hard incumbent — it is a category with five regional champions and no one in the middle.

What this changed in the plan

Two edits, both from evidence rather than taste. One: §3 gained a “long tail” row — fonts, certificates, SQLite, archives, email, calendars — because peek demonstrates each is a weekend of parsing plus a table, and together they are what makes a viewer feel universal rather than specialised. Two: §6 gained an explicit router in front of the document trait, with lazy pipeline loading, because flyfish shows how one binary can honestly claim 206 extensions without paying for them at startup. Neither change touches the size or startup budgets in §8 — both are the kind of thing you add because the budgets held.
